Source: `ch01-02_intro_probabilistic.txt`. Page refs are the **printed page numbers from the running headers** (Ch. 1 = pp. 3–46, Ch. 2 = pp. 47–80). The file's `(printed ~m)` markers are 2 too high (PDF 27 carries header "4", marker says "~6").

## 1. What these chapters are about

Chapter 1 argues, with small worked examples, that supervised ML is "an algorithmic form of statistical model estimation in which the data generation process is treated as an unknown" (p. 16). It contrasts ML with financial econometrics, introduces entropy and cross-entropy as the basis of model selection, defines over-fitting and regularization, sketches reinforcement learning, and ends with three practice examples: a momentum classifier, high-frequency execution, and mortgage models. Chapter 2 is a Bayesian primer: likelihood and MLE, bias and variance, Beta–Bernoulli updating, online learning, model evidence and Bayes factors, Occam's razor, Bayesian model averaging, and graphical models (naive Bayes ≡ logistic regression, mixtures, EM). The through-line for us: replace hard-wired rules with calibrated probabilities, select models by out-of-sample evidence, and respect small samples and non-stationarity.

## 2. Key claims and principles

**Statistical modelling vs ML (pp. 16–17, 29).** Statistics assumes "the data was generated by the model"; ML does not. "Traditional fit metrics, such as R², t-values, p-values, and the notion of statistical significance, are replaced by out-of-sample forecasting and understanding the bias–variance tradeoff" (p. 16). Table 1.2 (p. 17): statistics is causal, linear, "prone to over-fitting", with extensive diagnostics; ML is predictive, non-linear, "designed for out-of-sample performance", with limited diagnostics. They are "opposite ends of a continuum" with LASSO, ridge and Elastic Net "in the middle". Definition (p. 29): "regularized regression without use of parametric assumptions on the error distribution is an example of machine learning. Unregularized regression with, say, Gaussian error is not."

**Econometrics vs ML (pp. 5, 18–20, 30).** Econometric models "do not 'learn' the structure of the data"; with p features there are 2^p − p − 1 multiplicative interactions, and a decision tree "learns about the existence of the x1,t x2,t effect, yielding much better out-of-sample results" (p. 5). ARIMA/VAR momentum models "impose strong linearity" and "have strong bias which often causes underfitting" (p. 30). But ML engineering studies "overlook essential diagnostics such as Dickey–Fuller tests" (p. 18), and "RNNs like most methods in supervised machine learning are inherently designed for stationary data. Oftentimes, financial time series data is non-stationary" (p. 20).

**Fixed rules vs probabilities (pp. 10–13, 42).** Example 1.1: a rule table that reproduces four rows with probability 1.0 is "flawed if the actual relation between inputs and outputs is non-deterministic", yet "hard-wired rules such as this are ubiquitous in the finance industry such as rule-based technical analysis" (p. 11). A softer model h (0.9/0.05/0.05) fits with probability 0.9^4 = 0.6561 and "we can hardly exclude this model". "The heart of the model selection problem is that model g has excessively high confidence about the data, when that confidence is often not warranted" (p. 11). Exercise 1.2 (p. 42): in a betting game, models predicting with 100% certainty give the bettor **unbounded** losses; the 50/50 model's losses are **bounded**. Minimizing cross-entropy "or equivalently maximizing log-likelihood gives the maximum likelihood estimate" (p. 13).

**Over-fitting and bias–variance (pp. 21, 47, 49, 55, 63).** "The pivotal concern with machine learning, and especially deep learning, is the propensity for over-fitting" (p. 21). "Regularization is arguably the most important aspect of why machine learning methods have been so successful in finance… its absence is why neural networks fell out-of-favor in the finance industry in the 1990s." L2 ≡ Gaussian prior, L1 ≡ Laplacian prior; Bayesian models "have a natural mechanism for prevention of over-fitting built-in" (p. 21). MSE = variance + bias² (p. 55). "Virtually all specification tests used by frequentists hinge on asymptotic theory. This is a major limitation when the data size is finite" (p. 49). Small samples: "the choice between Bayesian and classical estimation often distills to a choice between trusting the asymptotic properties of estimators and trusting one's priors" (p. 63). Neural nets "tend to be very data intensive" (p. 47).

**Model selection: Bayes, Occam, many models, averaging (pp. 63–70).** Breiman's three linear models with RSS within 1% but disjoint variables: the "Rashomon effect" (pp. 63–64). Frequentist comparison needs nested models; Bayesian does not (p. 64). Model evidence is "the probability that the model could have generated the observed data, under the chosen prior" (p. 66). "Model selection is always relative rather than absolute. We must always pick a reference model" (p. 67). Example 2.4 (pp. 67–68): 115 heads in 200; Bayes factor for the fair coin is 1.2, |ln B| = 0.182, "no evidence", while a frequentist two-sided test rejects at p ≈ 4%. Occam (p. 69): "models that are too complex can generate many possible data sets, but they are unlikely to generate any particular dataset at random. Bayesian inference therefore automates the determination of model complexity… and does not need special 'fixes' (a.k.a regularization and information criteria)". BMA (pp. 69–70): "we are never fully convinced that a single model is the correct one"; "we often choose BMA when there is not strong enough evidence for any particular model."

**Algorithmic-trading example (pp. 29–32).** S&P 500, daily closes, ten years. Features: 5-day realized returns of every stock (Eq. 1.19); "returns are scale-free and no further standardization is needed" (p. 31); treat "a feature as a fixed symbol" because membership changes (fn. 8). Label (Eq. 1.18, p. 30): G_t = 1 if the equal-weight portfolio's mean h-day return minus the index return ≥ ε, else 0, h = 5. Model: feedforward net; training and evaluation deferred to Ch. 4. **No results are reported.** Rules: "The most complex practical aspect of machine learning is how to choose the input ('features') and output… it must be actionable (i.e., tradable)" (p. 30). "ε is typically chosen to be a small value so that the labels are not too imbalanced. As the value ε is increased, the problem becomes an 'outlier prediction problem'… beyond an off-the-shelf classifier" (p. 32). "A common mistake is to assume that building a predictive model will result in a profitable trading strategy" (p. 32).

**HFT execution example (pp. 32–34).** Market-maker adverse selection after a "price-flip". Features: "queue sizes at each price level" plus market-order properties; no distributional or Markov assumptions (p. 33). Model: RNN for mid-price direction (Ch. 8). No numbers. "The ability to accurately predict does not imply profitability… queue position, exchange matching rules, latency, position constraints, and price impact are central" (p. 34).

**Mortgage example (pp. 34–40): the non-stationarity lesson.** A model fit on 2001 loans matched actuals "almost perfectly" (p. 39); on 2006 loans it "predicted a decline of 25%, but the actual decline was approximately 56%" because lending standards changed and no data series captured them. "Cross-validation would not help here, as we cannot draw testing data from the distribution we care about, since that distribution comes from the future" (p. 39). Mislabelling 9→P vs 9→D "resulted in systematic over-prediction of prepayments" (p. 36). Known-useless collinear variables are excluded "to avoid over-fitting" (p. 37). "Low-dimensional projections… are extremely deceptive" (p. 40).

## 3. Formulas and procedures worth implementing

- Binary classifier output and its variance (p. 10): Ĝ = g₁(x|θ) = E[G | X=x, θ]; σ² = g₁ − g₁², "an inverted parabola with a maximum at g₁ = 0.5".
- Binary entropy (Eq. 1.6, p. 12): H = −p log₂ p − (1−p) log₂(1−p) ≤ 1 bit. Cross-entropy (Eq. 1.9, p. 13): H(f,g) = −p log₂ p_θ − (1−p) log₂(1−p_θ) ≥ H(f); worked: p = 0.7, p_θ = 0.68 → 0.8826322.
- Cross-entropy loss for binary labels (Ex. 1.4, p. 43; Eq. 2.47, p. 77): E(θ) = −Σᵢ [Gᵢ ln g₁(xᵢ|θ) + (1−Gᵢ) ln g₀(xᵢ|θ)]. Ex. 1.4 asks for the variant with πᵢ = probability label i is correct, which "renders the model robust to incorrectly labeled data"; the closed form is not given.
- Bernoulli MLE (p. 53): θ̂ = Σyᵢ/n, unbiased, Var = θ(1−θ)/n (p. 54). MSE = Var[θ̂] + bias² (p. 55).
- Bayes' rule (Eq. 2.1, p. 49): p(θ|y,X) ∝ p(θ) p(y|θ,X). Five steps (p. 56): model as a family conditional on θ; prior; insert data; Bayes' rule; "criticize your model and revise".
- Beta–Bernoulli update (p. 60): prior Beta(α,β) → posterior Beta(α + Σxᵢ, β + n − Σxᵢ); E[θ|x] = (α + Σxᵢ)/(α + β + n). Worked: α=β=2, 12/50 → 14/54 ≈ 0.259. Uniform prior (p. 58): Beta(12,38), mean 0.24, Var = 12·38/(50²·51) = 0.00357647, sd 0.0598.
- Sequential = batch (pp. 61–62): each posterior is the next prior; online form Eq. 2.7. Prediction (Eq. 2.8): p(y′|y) = ∫ p(y′|y,θ) p(θ|y) dθ.
- Model evidence and Bayes factor (Eqs. 2.16–2.20, pp. 65–66): p(Mᵢ|x) = p(Mᵢ)p(x|Mᵢ)/Σⱼ p(x|Mⱼ)p(Mⱼ); B₁,₂ = p(x|M₁)/p(x|M₂); log B = log p(x|M₁) − log p(x|M₂). Example 2.4 (p. 67): fair-coin evidence C(200,115)/2²⁰⁰ ≈ 0.005956; uniform-θ evidence = 1/201 ≈ 0.004975. Jeffreys' scale (Table 2.1) is an image; values not in the text.
- BMA prediction (Eq. 2.28, p. 70): p(y*|y) = Σᵢ p(y*|y,Mᵢ) p(Mᵢ|y).
- Logistic regression as posterior (Eqs. 2.29–2.31, pp. 70–71): σ(wX+b) is the Bayes posterior only if the Xⱼ are "conditionally independent over G; otherwise, the outputs from the logistic regression are not the true posterior probabilities".
- Mean–variance optimal fraction (Eq. 1.17, p. 28): u*_t = E[φ_t|S_t] / (2λ Var[φ_t|S_t]), from reward R_t = u_tφ_t − λu_t²Var[φ_t|S_t] (Eq. 1.15). A sizing rule given a predicted mean and variance of the trade return.

## 4. Pitfalls and warnings (beyond those quoted above)

- Principle of indifference: "Saying 'the probabilities of the outcomes are equally likely' contains strictly more information than 'I don't know'"; long and short payoffs are not symmetric (p. 59).
- Priors "that place substantial probability mass on practically infeasible ranges" mislead small-sample inference (p. 63, fn. 3).
- Mortgage models must be "built on regressors that themselves can be accurately predicted, or at least hedged" (p. 37): model stability depends on feature stability.
- Supervised learning "cannot capture the effect of market impact" (p. 34).
- "Statistical tests must be used to characterize the data and guide the choice of algorithm, such as, for example, tests for stationary" (p. 40). ML "is not a panacea" (p. 40); of the 1990s hype firms, "not a sausage of them" survived (p. 29).

## 5. Applicability to Ride The Wave

**Label design.** Use the "actionable (i.e., tradable)" label (p. 30): the sign of the P/L our own exits would produce. MFE/MAE and "+1% before −1%" are diagnostics; keep them and check they agree, because labels that are wins for the wrong reason bias everything (p. 36). Check the base rate of the ±1% label among the 8,370 candidates: if it is far from balanced we are in the "outlier prediction problem" the book says needs special handling (p. 32). Train and score with log-loss, not accuracy (p. 13, Ex. 1.2); Ex. 1.4's label-confidence weighting is the pointer for noisy labels.

**Model choice for ~8k rows.** No neural networks ("very data intensive", p. 47; sample size is a bias–variance question, p. 30 fn. 7). Start in the "middle of the continuum" (p. 16): L1/L2-regularized logistic regression, which is the MAP of a Bayesian logistic model (p. 21). Add a shallow tree ensemble as the book's answer to interactions (p. 5): the three gates are hand-coded interactions of session return, SPY return and trade-count ratio, and 12 features admit 2¹² − 12 − 1 = 4,083 candidate interactions. Drop or shrink collinear pairs (streak gain vs gain-vs-range; SPY 5-min vs session return) per p. 37. The reference model is the 3-gate rule (p. 67); if no model clearly wins, average them (Eq. 2.28, p. 70).

**Validation.** Walk-forward first-half/second-half is the right shape, but p. 39 says CV cannot sample the future and 75 days is one regime. Add: feature drift/stationarity checks between halves (pp. 20, 40); evaluation on the gated strategy's PF, hit rate and information ratio because prediction ≠ profit (p. 34); and Bayesian quantification of the result. With uniform prior the hit-rate posterior is Beta(wins+1, losses+1) (p. 58); at 116 trades its sd is ≈ sqrt(p(1−p)/116) ≈ 0.046 at p = 0.5. PF 1.25 on 116 trades is Example 2.4 territory (p. 68): compute the Bayes factor for "gated win rate = ungated win rate" before claiming the gates work.

**Calibration and sizing.** Logistic outputs are true posteriors only under conditional independence of features (p. 71); ours are correlated, so calibrate on the held-out half and report a calibration curve, not just AUC. Since variance g₁ − g₁² peaks at 0.5 (p. 10), a probability of 0.55 carries almost no information: gate well above 0.5 and size by Eq. 1.17 (predicted mean / (2λ · predicted variance)), capped, rather than linearly in p. Keep a running Beta–Bernoulli posterior of live win rate (pp. 61–62): two integers, exact, and a calibrated kill switch.

**Arguments against what we do now.** (1) The gates were chosen by sweeping and reading PF on the same 75 days: in-sample selection by a fit score, the Rashomon setup (pp. 63–64) plus over-fitting (p. 21); many gate sets will be "within 1%". (2) Deterministic gates are the "rule-based technical analysis" the book calls objectionable (p. 11); the classifier is the fix only if it outputs calibrated probabilities. (3) Reading "positive both halves" as proof is the p ≈ 4% trap (p. 68). (4) Expecting a Jun–Sep fit to hold in a new regime contradicts p. 39: plan re-fitting and drift monitoring.
