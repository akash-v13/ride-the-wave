# Dixon, Halperin & Bilokon, *Machine Learning in Finance* — Ch. 9 (Intro to RL) and Ch. 10 (Applications of RL)

Notes for a Ride The Wave skill. Page numbers are the book's printed pages (Ch. 9 = pp. 279–345, Ch. 10 = pp. 347–418). Quotes are verbatim.

## 1. What these chapters are about

Chapter 9 sets up Markov Decision Processes, Bellman equations and classical dynamic programming (policy/value iteration), then the sample-based RL methods (Monte Carlo, TD, SARSA, Q-learning, Fitted Q Iteration, LSPI, a short DQN section), illustrated with three toy finance MDPs: "financial cliff walking", optimal stock execution with market impact, and electronic market making. Chapter 10 works three real problems as RL: option hedging/pricing in discrete time (QLBS, solved by DP and by Fitted Q Iteration), multi-asset portfolio optimisation with signals, impact and costs (G-learning, an entropy-regularised Q-learning that collapses to a probabilistic LQR), and wealth management (Merton consumption, a defined-contribution retirement plan). The recurring engineering message: keep the reward quadratic (mean minus lambda times variance), keep the state low-dimensional, solve backward in time by least-squares regression on a basis, and prefer batch/off-policy learning from logged data over online exploration.

## 2. Key claims with page refs

**RL vs supervised learning.** In RL "a teacher provides only a partial feedback" via numerical rewards; the goal is "to maximize a total cumulative reward over a sequence of steps" (p. 280). Two things absent from supervised learning: a feedback loop from actions to the environment ("The presence of a feedback loop and the need for planning are unique to reinforcement learning", p. 281) and the exploration–exploitation dilemma (p. 281). In finance the loop is "trades changing the state of the order book" (p. 281) or market impact (p. 284).

**When RL collapses to something simpler.** "If rewards obtained at different steps are independent of each other ... the agent should simply pick the action that maximizes its local reward" (p. 284). With no feedback loop the multi-step problem "becomes equivalent to a sequence of independent one-step episodes", a contextual bandit; Thompson sampling is suggested for "intra-day trading, where arms would correspond to different trading strategies, or different specifications of the same trading strategy" (pp. 282–283).

**Batch (offline) RL is the finance setting.** Exploration "is only applicable in a regime of 'real-time' or on-line reinforcement learning"; in batch mode "the agent cannot explore, therefore the exploration-exploitation dilemma does not arise" and the task "is essentially a problem of inference of best possible actions given batch data of a recorded sequence of states, actions, and rewards" (p. 282). Off-policy methods matter because logged actions are rarely optimal and drift makes old data off-policy: "there are more examples of off-policy learning in real-world applications than of on-policy learning" (p. 315).

**MDP, value and Q functions.** MDP = states, actions, p(s'|s,a), rewards, discount gamma (pp. 289–290). V^pi(s) = expected discounted reward sum from s (eq. 9.10, p. 293); Q^pi(s,a) fixes the first action (eq. 9.12, p. 294); for episodic tasks gamma = 1 is fine (p. 293). The Bellman equation V_t(s) = E[R_t + gamma V_{t+1}(s')] is linear and "can be solved backward in time, starting with t = T − 1" (p. 295). Bellman optimality Q*_t(s,a) = E[R_t + gamma max_a' Q*_{t+1}(s',a')] (eq. 9.20, p. 297) is non-linear; given Q*, act by argmax_a (p. 298). "finite-horizon MDP problems are more common in finance", e.g. "a daily fund performance can result from multiple trades executed on a minute scale during the day" (p. 296); finite horizon = augment the state with time, (s, t) (p. 299). "financial markets often have longer memories than 10 time steps" (p. 287), so the state must summarise history; the book stays with fully observed MDPs (pp. 288, 293).

**DP feasibility and data requirements.** DP needs a known model and few states; discretised continuous states are feasible only up to "three or four" dimensions (pp. 300, 304). RL "cannot aim at an exact solution" and seeks a "'sufficiently good' solution" (pp. 306–307). Monte Carlo needs "a complete T-step trajectory" per sample (p. 308); TD(0) learns from "a single observation!" and is "very volatile" (pp. 311–312). Fitted Q with K basis functions needs "at least K observations (and, to avoid high variance estimations, a multiple of this number)" per time step (p. 327). "The practice of using Deep Q-learning on finance problems is problematic. The sheer number of parameters that need to be tuned and configured renders the approach much more complex than Q-learning, LSPI" (p. 336). "Low signal-to-noise ratios and potentially very high dimensionality are therefore two marked differences" of finance from games and robotics (p. 348).

**Reward design.** Every finance example uses R = return − lambda·Var[return] (pp. 285, 289, 320; QLBS eq. 10.35, p. 363; portfolio eq. 10.85, p. 384). One-step variance penalties make a risk-sensitive problem "amenable to a standard risk-neutral approach of MDP models" (p. 387; "risk-sensitive reinforcement learning", p. 364). Rewards must be bounded (p. 301). Reward shaping r~ = r + gamma f(s') − f(s) leaves the optimal policy unchanged (Ex. 9.5, p. 339). Quadratic penalties "are symmetric" (p. 406).

**Ch. 9 mini-cases.** Financial cliff walking (pp. 305, 316): balance x time grid 4 x 12, actions deposit/withdraw/hold, tabular SARSA and Q-learning with tapered epsilon converge to the same policy. Optimal execution (pp. 319–321): state = inventory (10) x price bucket (12) x time (10) = 1,200 cells, actions sell {0,1,2,3} blocks, linear impact, reward n a_t S_t − lambda n Var[S_{t+1} X_{t+1}], Q stored as a 10 x 12 x 10 x 4 tensor. Market making (pp. 322–323): state = inventory {−1,0,1} x 5 liquidity-ratio buckets = 15 states, actions bid/ask/hold, fill probability = liquidity ratio, reward expected P&L net of a sub-tick cost; caveat: no queue position or cancels. LSPI allocation (pp. 333–334): 2,000 simulated paths, T = 10, 256 B-spline basis functions, recovers the analytic Markowitz weight.

**Ch. 10 QLBS option hedging (pp. 349–380).** State X_t = de-trended log price (eq. 10.26, p. 361); action = hedge ratio a_t; reward eq. 10.35 = gamma a_t dS_t − lambda Var[hedge portfolio] (p. 363); terminal Q_T = −Pi_T − lambda Var[Pi_T] (eq. 10.41, p. 365). Reward is quadratic in a_t and the hedge does not move the price, so the optimal action is analytic (eq. 10.45, p. 366) and "the action in this equation is always just one optimal action" (p. 367); price = −Q*(S, a*) (eq. 10.48, p. 367). DP = two least-squares solves per step (eqs. 10.53, 10.57, pp. 369–370). RL = Fitted Q Iteration on tuples (X_t, a_t, R_t, X_{t+1}) (eq. 10.58, p. 370), Q quadratic in a via a 3 x M coefficient matrix (eq. 10.59, p. 371), W_t = S_t^{-1} M_t (eq. 10.63, p. 372). Data and results: S0 = 100, mu = 0.05, sigma = 0.15, T = 1y, ATM put, bi-weekly re-hedge, **50,000 MC paths, 12 cubic B-splines, lambda = 0.001**, ridge 1e-3 on the inversion; QLBS price 4.90 ± 0.12 vs BS 4.53; on-policy FQI equals DP (pp. 373–374). Off-policy with hedges multiplied by U[1−eta, 1+eta], eta up to 0.5: "the impact of sub-optimality of actions in recorded data is rather mild" (p. 374). Caveats: quadratic utility can give negative fair prices (p. 357); no costs or impact (p. 365); multi-asset bases are "intractable beyond a rather low (< 10) number of dimensions" (p. 380); proxy data "will just slow down the learning, so that more data would be needed" (p. 378).

**Ch. 10 G-learning portfolios (pp. 380–401).** State = (dollar positions, signals z_t); action = dollar trades; reward = expected excess return − lambda variance − impact − fees, quadratic (eqs. 10.88–10.92, pp. 384–385). A signal is worth including only if "(i) it correlates with equity returns, (ii) is predictable itself ... (iii) its characteristic times tau are larger than the time step" (p. 383). Stochastic policies because logged trades are "sub-optimal, or noisy at times, because of model mis-specifications, market timing lags, human errors" (p. 387); KL penalty to a Gaussian reference policy with inverse temperature beta "tuned to the level of noise in the data" (p. 390); with zero impact the solution is an entropy-regularised LQR in closed form (eqs. 10.134–10.144, pp. 397–399); with impact the dynamics are non-linear and need iterative linearisation (pp. 395, 401). No empirical or out-of-sample results are reported.

**Ch. 10 wealth management (pp. 401–412).** Merton/CRRA closed form (Example 10.9, pp. 402–404). Defined-contribution plan: reward −c_t − lambda E[(target − value)_+] − costs, approximated quadratically (eqs. 10.154–10.156, pp. 405–406); same G-learning recursion; Fig. 10.7 is a 100-asset, 30-step simulation (p. 412).

## 3. Procedures worth implementing

**(a) Longstaff–Schwartz continuation-value regression for stop/continue (pp. 354–355; early-exercise extension p. 379).** The one recipe here that casts hold-vs-sell as a small MDP solvable without learning.
1. Take N forward paths, simulated or "real historical data ... together with a pre-determined hedging strategy" (p. 353). Because the decision does not affect the path, "such simulation of forward paths should only be performed once, and then re-used" (p. 353).
2. Binary control "exercise" / "not exercise". Continuation value C_t(S_t) = E[e^{−r dt} max(h_{t+dt}(S_{t+dt}), C_{t+dt}(S_{t+dt})) | F_t] (eq. 10.6), h = immediate payoff.
3. Read eq. 10.6 as the regression max(h_{t+dt}, C_{t+dt}) = e^{r dt} C_t(S_t) + eps_t (eq. 10.7); expand C_t(x) = sum_n a_n(t) phi_n(x) (eq. 10.8); fit a_n(t) by least squares across all paths alive at t.
4. Go backward t = T−1, ..., 0; stop when h_t(S_t) > C_t(S_t) ("comparing its value from the next time step continued to the current time step with an intrinsic option value", p. 379).

**(b) Fitted Q Iteration (pp. 327–328; QLBS form pp. 370–372).** Data = tuples (s, a, r, s'); basis psi_k(s,a). For t = T−1 down to 0 regress y = r + gamma max_a' Q_{t+1}(s',a') on psi(s,a) (eq. 9.58); W_t = S_t^{-1} M_t with S = sum psi psi^T, M = sum psi y (eq. 10.64); add a small ridge (p. 374). Take the inner max from the previous step's fitted Q, never from the empirical max in the same data (pp. 328–329, 372).

**(c) Tabular finite-horizon Q / value iteration (pp. 303, 314, 316–321).** Q_t(s,a) as a state x time x action tensor; Q(s,a) <- Q(s,a) + alpha [r + gamma max_a' Q_{t+1}(s',a') − Q(s,a)] (eq. 9.46) with decaying alpha and tapered epsilon; or with empirical transition counts, V(s) = max_a sum_s' p(s'|s,a)[r + gamma V(s')] (eq. 9.32).

**(d) Risk-adjusted reward template.** R_t = realised return − lambda·Var[next-step value | state] (pp. 285, 289, 363, 384); QLBS used lambda = 0.001 on a price near 100.

**(e) Thompson sampling over strategy variants (pp. 282–283).** Binomial bandit with a sigmoid link on context; sample theta from the posterior, play the best arm, Bayesian update.

## 4. Pitfalls and warnings

- Overestimation bias: an empirical max in place of E[max Q] "can lead to distorted and sometimes even diverging value functions" (pp. 328–329); fitting action and Q on the same data "would be wrong" (p. 372). Fix: analytic/previous-step action or Double Q-learning.
- Linear architectures give "no guidance on how to choose a good set of basis functions" (p. 327); cross-product bases explode beyond ~3–4 (DP) or ~10 (QLBS) dimensions (pp. 300, 304, 380).
- Non-Markov memory: the state must carry the relevant history (p. 287).
- Noise: low signal-to-noise (p. 348); finite-sample value estimates "can become quite noisy themselves" (p. 380); single-observation TD updates are volatile (p. 312).
- Bounded rewards (p. 301); symmetric quadratic penalties (p. 406); negative prices under quadratic utility (p. 357).
- Learning-rate and epsilon schedules are "specific to a problem" (p. 318); SARSA and Q-learning agree only under tapered epsilon (p. 316, Ex. 9.8 p. 340); non-non-expansion softmax operators make SARSA oscillate (Ex. 9.15, pp. 343–344).
- Signals with characteristic time shorter than the decision step are "indistinguishable from the white noise" (p. 383).
- Deep Q-learning in finance is "problematic" (p. 336).

## 5. Applicability to Ride The Wave

**A better exit rule: yes, via 3(a).** Our exit is an American-style optimal stopping problem with a binary control, and our trades do not move the price, so the forward paths already exist in the feature dataset. Episode = one entry candidate; time = minutes since entry with a finite horizon (60-minute cap or 3:55 pm flatten); state = a 2–3 dimensional summary such as (unrealised gain from entry, drawdown from the running peak, minutes held), which is the Markov summary the current trailing rule already conditions on; payoff h_t = unrealised gain net of slippage and commission (the stop is h at the stop price). Fit C_t across paths alive at each minute on a small basis (the book used 12 B-splines in 1-D on 50,000 paths; with 8,370 paths and ~60 steps use a handful of terms per dimension plus a ridge, p. 374). Exit rule: sell when h_t > C_t(state). No simulator, exploration or reward model is needed; this is DP on logged paths, the batch setting the authors say fits finance (p. 282). The risk-adjusted variant (3(d), subtract lambda times next-minute variance) is a principled replacement for the hard-coded minimum-gain floor. Ex. 9.5 (p. 339) implies that paying the change in unrealised P&L each minute or a lump sum at exit gives the same optimal policy with gamma = 1, so either reward bookkeeping works (my inference from the reward-shaping result).

**Tabular sanity check (3(c)).** Like the 15-state market-making example (p. 322): bucket gain x drawdown x minutes held into ~50–100 cells, estimate transitions from the 446k rows, run finite-horizon value iteration with {hold, sell}. The authors recommend exactly this DP cross-check for a function-approximation solution (p. 300).

**What an honest RL attempt would need.** Data: (state, action, reward, next state) per minute; we have them, and because the price path is exogenous the counterfactual "sell now" reward is observable at every minute, which removes the usual off-policy coverage problem for the exit. Simulator: not needed for stopping; needed only for entries or sizing with impact, which is where the authors say non-linearity and real difficulty begin (pp. 395, 401). Reward: mean minus lambda variance per minute, bounded (p. 301). Method: FQI with a linear basis, backward in hold-time, train on the first half of days and test on the second; not DQN (p. 336). Realistic: yes as a DP/LSM study on the existing dataset; no as end-to-end learning of the whole strategy. Budget comparison: 50,000 simulated paths for a 1-D state and quadratic Q in the book vs our 8,370 real, noisier episodes argues for a very small basis and expecting a "sufficiently good" (p. 307) gain, probably in fewer stop-outs rather than more upside.

**What argues against what we do.**
- Signal test (p. 383): a 3-bar streak has a characteristic time of a few minutes on a 1-minute step and is not obviously "predictable itself"; the authors would want a feature that persists longer than the decision interval. Consistent with 62% of entries hitting the stop.
- Bandit view (pp. 282–284): if entries are near-independent one-shot bets, the strategy-selection problem is a contextual bandit over parameter profiles (Thompson sampling across `config/profiles/`), not an MDP; the MDP framing is justified only for the intra-trade hold/sell sequence.
- Overestimation (pp. 328–329, 372): an exit rule tuned and evaluated on the same 8,370 episodes will look better than it is; keep the second-half holdout.
- Low signal-to-noise (p. 348): do not expect DP on exits to move PF 1.25 far.

## 6. What to skip and why

- QLBS derivations (Sections 3.3–4.5, pp. 355–372) beyond the LSM box and the FQI template: continuous hedge ratio for a European option; our action is binary on one stock.
- G-learning / entropy-regularised LQR (Sections 5.8–5.16, pp. 386–401) and the adversarial interpretation (pp. 392–393): multi-asset dollar allocation with impact and Gaussian policies; we trade one name at a time with no impact.
- Merton consumption and the retirement plan (Section 6, pp. 401–412): cash-flow planning.
- Non-expansion operators, mellowmax, Boltzmann policies (pp. 329–330, 343–344): only relevant to soft-max exploration, which batch DP does not need.
- Deep RL (pp. 335–336): the authors advise against it for finance.
